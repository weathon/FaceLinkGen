[Intro]
This episode is a close read of the paper introducing FaceLinkGen, an attack on privacy-preserving face recognition systems — systems designed to let you get matched against a face database without ever handing over your actual photo. Its one-line thesis: the field has been checking privacy by asking whether an attacker can rebuild your photo pixel for pixel, and that's the wrong test, because looking similar and being the same identity are not the same thing. This episode dwells on the reasoning behind that claim — how identity gets formalized as separable from a photo's incidental details, how a model distills just that signal out of a template never meant to give it up, the resulting numbers against three published systems, and what happens when the same idea is turned on systems built to do the opposite: hide identity while keeping a face looking human.

[Setting the stage: PPFR and the reconstruction-only blind spot]
Privacy-Preserving Face Recognition, or PPFR, systems exist to solve a specific discomfort: to get matched against a face database, you currently have to hand over your actual face. PPFR tries to break that dependency. Before your image ever leaves your device, it gets converted into what the field calls a protected template — a transformed representation that a recognition server can still compare against other templates to confirm identity, but that is supposed to resist being inverted back into your original photo, and to resist yielding side information like your age or ethnicity to whoever holds it. The pitch is: same recognition utility, none of the raw biometric exposure.

The paper's starting point is a critique of how the field has been checking whether that pitch actually holds. Look at the line of recent frequency-domain PPFR systems — PartialFace, MinusFace, FracFace, DuetFace, FaceObfuscator — and you find a shared evaluation habit. Privacy is operationalized as resistance to pixel-level reconstruction: an attacker tries to rebuild the original image from the protected template, and success or failure is scored with PSNR and SSIM, peak signal-to-noise ratio and structural similarity index, two image-quality metrics imported wholesale from the compression and image-quality literature. If an attacker's reconstruction scores low on these metrics, the template is declared private. That's the entire evaluation paradigm across five representative systems.

The authors' central objection is that this conflates two different things: how similar an image looks at the pixel level, and whether it belongs to the same identity. Those are not the same axis. It's entirely possible to generate two images that score very high on pixel similarity while depicting two different people, and equally possible for two photographs of the same person — an ID photo and a casual social media shot, say — to score low on pixel similarity while being trivially the same identity to any recognition system. If that's true, then a template that successfully resists pixel reconstruction has told you nothing definitive about whether identity leaked through it.

This is why they build FaceLinkGen, an attack that skips pixel reconstruction as the goal entirely and instead tries to distill identity directly out of the protected template. The headline numbers are worth previewing: across three recent frequency-domain PPFR methods, FaceLinkGen achieves 72 to 83 percent recall on 1-to-N linkage — correctly matching a template to the right identity among N candidates — 98.5 percent accuracy on 1-to-1 matching, and better than 96 percent success regenerating a recognizable face. The same approach, turned around, also breaks face anonymization systems that have the opposite goal. Those numbers are what the rest of this episode unpacks.

That's the critique in outline; the paper backs it with a very concrete demonstration of pixel similarity and identity similarity pulling in opposite directions on the same images.

[The core empirical claim: pixel similarity is not identity similarity]
The evidence for this starts with a figure showing two pairs of face images side by side, and it is worth walking through the numbers directly. In the first pair, the two faces look almost identical at the pixel level: SSIM of 0.86 and PSNR of 28.0 — high scores on both of the pixel-fidelity metrics just introduced, meaning the images could pass for near-duplicates. Yet when you run these same two faces through an identity-similarity score, which the paper labels FS and which comes from comparing face-recognition embeddings rather than pixels, the pair scores only 0.185 — read as two different people. The second pair runs the opposite direction: SSIM of 0.35 and PSNR of 12.6, pixel metrics low enough that a human glancing at raw difference maps would call the images unrelated, but an FS of 0.729, which reads as a strong identity match. Pixel similarity and identity similarity are not just imperfectly correlated here — they point in opposite directions on the same pair of examples.

Table I turns this into a controlled comparison using CanFG, a protection method that deterministically remaps every face to a different synthetic identity. Compare a CanFG-protected output to some unrelated face, and you get SSIM 0.235, PSNR 10.44, MSE — mean squared error, the raw pixel-difference term PSNR is built from — of 6699, and FS 0.586. Now compare that same protected output back to the original source face it was generated from: pixel similarity jumps sharply, SSIM 0.841, PSNR 26.81, MSE down to 143, looking every bit like a successful reconstruction. But FS collapses to 0.008, essentially zero identity correspondence. The protected image is pixel-close to its source and identity-far from it, simultaneously.

The reason this matters is what it implies about where identity actually lives in an image: it argues for treating identity information, notated z_I, and everything else — pose, lighting, expression, the nuisance factors — notated z_N, as separable components rather than one entangled signal. Standard Model Inversion Attacks, which try to recover the original pixels from an embedding, and reconstruction-centric evaluation more broadly, are effectively optimizing for z_N. A protected template was never designed to retain that nuisance information, so of course reconstruction fails against it — but that failure says nothing about whether z_I, the part that actually identifies the person, survived.

That z_I versus z_N split is a claim about what an image contains. Before pushing the formal argument further, the paper pauses to define precisely who is doing the extracting — the adversary this whole attack assumes.

[Threat model: who is the adversary, and why insider access is the honest framing]
Every attack in this paper stands or falls on who you assume the enemy is, so it's worth dwelling on how FaceLinkGen frames its adversary. PPFR started from one well-defined worry: Erkin and colleagues in 2009 motivated the whole paradigm around a user who must hand a face image to a remote recognition server but does not trust that server. Not the network -- the server. Whoever legitimately receives the protected template is the party the protection scheme exists to constrain: the curious-or-malicious service provider, an insider by definition. Eavesdropping on the wire is a separate, already-closed problem -- TLS handles interception in transit, and the paper is explicit this was never what PPFR set out to answer.

The paper's complaint is that some recent PPFR work quietly swaps that adversary out. It singles out Mi et al.'s description of the attacker as "typically envisioned as a malicious third-party wiretapping the transmission," an external framing that moves the goalposts away from the server itself. The pushback runs two ways. First, even if the conversion step producing the protected template runs locally on the user's device, the service provider can still feed arbitrary faces through that local client and collect the input-output pairs, reverse-engineering the conversion through oracle access without touching its internals. Second, moving conversion to a trusted third-party server doesn't remove the trust problem, only relocates it: the user must now trust that third party with raw face data, exactly what PPFR was invented to eliminate.

Against that backdrop, FaceLinkGen assumes an attacker who knows less than the threat models in the papers it's attacking. It needs no knowledge of the conversion architecture, parameters, or hyperparameters -- only the ability to query the conversion process with chosen inputs and observe the protected outputs. That's weaker than what those papers grant their own adversary: FaceObfuscator assumes the attacker knows the conversion architecture, even without the randomly-selected channel parameters; PartialFace assumes access to the specific channel identifiers selected during protection; CanFG rests its entire security case on the conversion model's secrecy, an assumption the reverse-engineering point above undercuts, since paired original-protected data, once obtainable, suffices to train a substitute model.

Worth separating this from an earlier attack too. Zhang et al. previously broke protected templates, but their method needed roughly 6,900 online verification queries per identity and depended on the server returning continuous similarity scores to optimize against -- demanding, since most deployed systems return only a binary accept-or-reject decision or a quantized score, and operators can rate-limit that pattern. FaceLinkGen's attack doesn't touch the server at all, which is the point of grounding the whole analysis in oracle access to the conversion process rather than in the recognition service's behavior.

With that weaker, oracle-only adversary established, the next step is formalizing exactly what such an attacker is trying to pull out of a template.

[Formalizing the attack: distilling identity out of a protected template]
Let's formalize what "identity information" even means here, because the whole method hinges on a clean decomposition. Take a face image X and treat it as generated from two latent components: an identity factor z_I — everything that makes this face recognizably this person — and a nuisance factor z_N — pose, lighting, expression, background, all the incidental variation that changes across photos of the same individual without changing who they are. Formally, X is drawn from a conditional distribution given both z_I and z_N together. Now, when a transformation-based PPFR system builds its protected template T, the design goal is to keep z_I while stripping out z_N: T is drawn from a distribution conditioned on z_I alone, discarding the nuisance information entirely. That's Equation 1 in the paper, and it's worth sitting with, because it explains something the earlier section only demonstrated empirically: since z_N is deliberately and mostly discarded by construction, any attack that tries to reconstruct the full pixel-level image X back from T is attempting to invert information that was never retained in the first place. That's why full reconstruction is severely ill-posed, and why prior attacks — which try to recover specific nuisance factors like exact lighting or head pose that simply no longer exist in T — end up producing blurry, identity-inconsistent faces. They're solving the wrong problem.

FaceLinkGen sidesteps this by not trying to recover z_N at all. Instead, it runs a distillation procedure aimed squarely at z_I. The authors train a student model, f_s, whose job is to map the template T directly to an identity representation, z_I prime. To supervise this, they use a frozen teacher model, f_t, that produces embeddings from the original, unprotected images. The training objective, Equation 2, is simple: minimize one minus the average cosine similarity — a standard measure of angular closeness between two vectors — between the student's output on the template and the teacher's output on the corresponding original image, averaged across N training pairs. Both teacher and student are instantiated as ArcFace, a widely used, publicly available face embedding model. That choice isn't arbitrary; ArcFace is specifically compatible with Arc2Face, the generative model the attack later uses to turn a recovered identity vector back into a face. The paper is explicit that the method isn't tied to ArcFace: any embedding model — FaceNet is their example — would work, as long as there's a matching generative counterpart, since the actual recognition backbone running on the PPFR server is arbitrary and unknown to the attacker anyway.

[From identity vector to attack: linkage and regeneration]
With the recovered identity vector z_I' now in hand, the next question is what to do with it. The paper's answer is to skip pixel reconstruction entirely and instead hand z_I' to a diffusion-based generative model — a network that synthesizes images by iteratively denoising random noise, here conditioned on an identity embedding — specifically Arc2Face, denoted g_diff. The attacker samples a fresh vector of Gaussian noise, epsilon drawn from a standard normal distribution, and computes Y equals g_diff of z_I' and epsilon, equation 3 in the paper. The key move is what's absent from that equation: there is no attempt to recover the true nuisance factor z_N that the protection scheme deliberately discarded — the lighting, pose, background, whatever the transform suppressed. Instead the attacker substitutes freshly sampled noise for it. Since that sampled noise is not equal to the original z_N, this cannot be pixel-accurate reconstruction, and it isn't trying to be — it's closer in spirit to StyleGAN-style latent inversion, where you only need to land in the right region of a generative model's latent space, not recover an exact input. As long as the identity component survives intact inside the template, feeding it through the generator with any noise sample yields a face that reads as the same identity, even if lighting, pose, or framing differ from the original photo.

This substitution opens two distinct attack vectors, laid out in Figure 3. The first is linkage, which never touches the generator at all — everything happens in embedding space. Given a query face, compute its embedding, then search a database of protected templates for the one whose extracted embedding has the highest cosine similarity: formally, the argmax over templates t of s(e_q, f_s(t)), equation 4. This comes in two flavors: face-to-template, matching a real photo of a known individual against a leaked database of protected templates, and template-to-template, matching two protected templates from different leaked databases to reveal they belong to the same person. The paper flags a real gap here: the ISO/IEC 24745 biometric standard explicitly requires resistance against template-to-template search, but says nothing about face-to-template linkage — so a system can be standards-compliant while still letting an attacker tie a protected template to a named person from a public photo.

The second vector is regeneration: feed z_I' into Arc2Face and output an actual face image, one a recognition system would score as the same identity as the original enrollment photo, without ever reconstructing that photo's pixels.

With both attack vectors defined in principle, the paper turns to testing them against real, published systems.

[Experimental setup: three targets, three test datasets, negligible cost]
The paper doesn't test every frequency-domain PPFR method that's ever been published; it tests three, and the selection criterion is worth stating plainly: PartialFace from ICCV 2023, MinusFace from CVPR 2024, and FracFace from NeurIPS 2025, chosen specifically because all three ship open-source code. That's not a minor footnote — plenty of PPFR papers describe a frequency-masking or channel-shuffling scheme in enough detail to sound reproducible but never release the implementation, which makes independent attack evaluation impossible. The authors flag one wrinkle here: FracFace's published code has some gaps relative to what its paper describes, and rather than reconstructing the paper's intended method, they evaluated against the code as released, since that's what anyone downloading the repository would actually be running.

Training the student model that distills identity out of a protected template uses a subset of CASIA-WebFace — roughly ten thousand identities, about ninety thousand images, split eighty-twenty into train and validation. The identity-overlap question matters here: none of those training identities overlap with whatever the target PPFR systems were trained on. That's easy to guarantee for PartialFace and FracFace, because they're training-free — hardcoded frequency transforms with no learned weights and thus nothing to leak from. MinusFace is the one actual neural target, and it was trained on MS1Mv2, a separate corpus from CASIA-WebFace. The embedding backbone throughout is Antelopev2, with one added three-by-three convolutional layer bolted on front to absorb differences in template channel count across the three schemes. Antelopev2 specifically because it's the embedding space Arc2Face was built to accept as input.

For regeneration testing, three datasets, each doing a different job. The CASIA-WebFace hold-out set — 2,115 images — guarantees no identity leakage into the attack's own training data. TPDNE, "this person does not exist," is a synthetic-face dataset chosen because it keeps evaluation uncontaminated by anything Stable Diffusion 1.5 or Arc2Face saw during their own training, and its images happen to resemble the headshot-style photos typically used to enroll faces into these systems in the first place. LFW tests the harder case: generalization across a distribution shift the student model never saw during distillation.

And the cost, because it undercuts any argument that this attack is impractical to mount: distillation training finishes in under two hours per method on a single NVIDIA A6000, at an estimated eighty cents to a dollar sixty, with comparable or better wall-clock time on consumer GPUs like an RTX 4090 or 5090.

[Linkage results: recall near the theoretical ceiling]
Let's get to the numbers themselves, because they're the crux of the linkage claim. Table II reports closed-set top-one linkage recall — meaning: given a probe embedding, is the correct identity's match the single best-scoring hit among all candidates in the gallery, not just somewhere in the top five or top ten. The test population is a 2,115-image hold-out slice of CASIA-WebFace, held out specifically so no identity in it overlaps with anything the distillation model saw during training. The setup cross-matches four representations against each other as query and key: FracFace, MinusFace, PartialFace — the three protected schemes under attack — and the original, unprotected image embeddings, giving a four-by-four grid of linkage recall numbers.

Start with the diagonal case that matters most as a control: original-image-to-original-image linkage, embeddings from unprotected faces matched against other unprotected embeddings of the same identity. That number is 0.8823. Notice this isn't 1.0, and it can't be, because CASIA-WebFace is known from prior work to carry somewhere between 9.3 and 13.0 percent inherent label noise — some fraction of images are simply mislabeled to the wrong identity. So 0.8823 isn't a flaw in anyone's method; it's the practical ceiling imposed by the dataset itself, the best any linkage system could ever do here regardless of how good the embeddings are.

Against that ceiling, look at the cross-template and cross-domain numbers — protected embedding matched against a different protected scheme, or protected matched against original. They're consistently above 70 percent, and frequently push past 80: 0.8137 for FracFace-to-PartialFace, 0.7754 for MinusFace-to-PartialFace, 0.8563 for PartialFace-to-Original, among others in the table. These are template types built explicitly to prevent exactly this kind of cross-matching, and yet the attack lands within single digits of a theoretical maximum that's already discounted for dataset noise.

Table IV closes the loop on 1-to-1 verification, the traditional face-recognition benchmark: is this one template and this one face the same person, at the accuracy-maximizing threshold. MinusFace-to-Face scores 0.992 accuracy, 0.995 AUROC — the area under the true-positive-versus-false-positive curve as the decision threshold sweeps across its full range. FracFace-to-Face comes in at 0.988 and 0.993, PartialFace-to-Face at 0.992 and 0.996. The unprotected Face-to-Face baseline sits at 0.998 and 0.998. The gap between protected and unprotected verification is a rounding error — these protection schemes were not just breached at the identity-linkage level, they cost their systems almost nothing on the exact benchmark they were designed to preserve.

[Regeneration results and the U-Net baseline]
Turn now to the regeneration attack, a harder ask than linkage: not just whether a recovered embedding matches the right identity, but whether you can reconstruct a face from it. Evaluation runs on the first 1,000 images from each of three datasets — TPDNE, the CASIA-WebFace hold-out, and LFW. Each image is converted to a protected template and distilled to an identity embedding, as before. That embedding is handed to Arc2Face, an identity-conditioned face generator, producing five output images per embedding, since diffusion generation is stochastic and one draw can miss. Two numbers get reported per method per dataset: a straight per-image success rate, and Success at five — at least one of the five faces passing identity verification. Verification comes from Face++, a commercial face-matching API marketed at "financial-grade" security, evaluated at three false-acceptance-rate thresholds — one in a thousand, one in ten thousand, one in a hundred thousand — the strictest tolerating fewer than one wrongful match per hundred thousand genuine non-matches.

Table III lays out the results by target and dataset. On TPDNE, PartialFace, MinusFace, and FracFace post Success-at-five of 1.000, 0.996, and 0.992, with pass rates at the strictest threshold of 0.993, 0.936, and 0.904. The CASIA-WebFace hold-out gives 0.992, 0.989, 0.991 for Success-at-five and 0.957, 0.930, 0.920 at the tightest threshold. LFW comes in at 0.988, 0.987, 0.979, and 0.980, 0.974, 0.943. Across all nine method-dataset pairs, first-attempt success never falls below 97 percent, Success-at-five ranges from 97.9 up to a full 100 percent, and even the harshest threshold holds above 90 percent everywhere.

It is also cheap to run. Generating a batch of five images takes about three seconds on an A6000 GPU using the Stable Diffusion 1.5 backbone, which works out to roughly 1,200 identities recovered per hour, at an estimated cost near $0.0005 per regeneration.

The paper then checks itself against the reconstruction baseline that MinusFace and FracFace used in their own papers to argue their protections held: a U-Net trained from scratch with a pixel-wise L1 loss mapping template directly to the original image. That baseline fails to produce a valid face most of the time. So the comparison restricts to the subset of U-Net outputs that are both detected as real faces and pass verification — the most generous slice available to it. Even there, FaceLinkGen scores worse than U-Net on SSIM and PSNR while dominating it on identity metrics. The reconstruction that looks pixel-wise closer to the source is the one carrying less identity signal a commercial verifier will recognize.

[Cross-checking with a second vendor, and grading against the papers' own claims]
Before trusting Face++'s numbers too far, the authors ask an obvious question: what if Face++ itself is a lenient judge, and the whole regeneration attack's success is an artifact of testing against one commercial verifier? To close that gap, they bring in a second, independent opinion — Amazon's face comparison service, accessed through the EdenAI aggregator, run over a subset of 700 selected images from the LFW dataset. Amazon's API is coarser than Face++: it doesn't return a similarity score you can threshold yourself, only a binary pass or fail plus a confidence number, so there's no equivalent of picking the strictest FAR threshold here. Table V reports the resulting pass rates: 0.99 for PartialFace, 0.98 for MinusFace, 0.92 for FracFace. These track the Face++ numbers closely, which is the point — two commercial-grade verifiers, built by different companies on different training data, agree that the regenerated faces carry the same identity as the originals. That convergence is what rules out "this is just how Face++ happens to score things." Where the two verifiers diverge, the paper doesn't paper over it: Figure 4 shows actual examples where Amazon rejects a face that Face++ accepts, and the authors attribute this to Amazon simply running a stricter internal acceptance threshold, not to Amazon being more discerning about identity — EdenAI doesn't disclose Amazon's underlying false-accept-rate operating point, so a direct threshold-for-threshold comparison isn't possible.

The more consequential comparison in this section isn't between two verifiers, though — it's between FaceLinkGen's identity-centric evaluation and how these three methods graded themselves in their original papers. Take FracFace's own "Protection" percentage: it's defined purely as the fraction of frequency-domain channels that got filtered out or structurally disrupted during anonymization. That's a statement about how much of the transform was perturbed, not about whether a face verifier can still recognize the person underneath it. Table VII lays the two standards side by side. PartialFace reports 0.680 protection by its own channel-disruption metric, but under FaceLinkGen's actual attack, measured protection is 0.002 in a single trial and 0.000 averaged across five trials — effectively total leakage. MinusFace claims 0.850 protection, versus 0.011 and 0.004 once attacked. FracFace claims a perfect 1.000 — complete channel disruption — yet measured protection under attack comes out to only 0.015, falling to 0.008 across five trials. The pattern across all three methods is identical: aggressively disrupting the frequency-domain representation does not stop a student model from recovering the identity that representation still encodes.

[Stress-testing the attack: fewer images, transfer across methods, no shared secret]
Having established that the attack works under generous conditions, the authors turn to stripping away the assumptions one at a time, starting with attacker resources. The main results assumed a reasonably sized training set for the student model; here they ask what happens if an attacker has almost nothing. Working against FracFace, they cut the training set down to roughly 800 images, compensating with higher weight decay and a smaller batch size, both standard countermeasures against overfitting when data is scarce. The resulting training run finished in under fifty seconds. Despite that, the student model achieved a 97.0 percent generation pass rate at a false accept rate of one in one hundred thousand, and 99.5 percent linkage accuracy — numbers barely distinguishable from the full-data results reported earlier. Pushing further, they dropped the training set to just 256 images and still got 98.7 percent linkage accuracy and 90.5 percent regeneration success. The implication is that identity information in these templates isn't diffuse or hard to find — it's concentrated enough that even a handful of images lets a small model triangulate it.

The second stress test asks a harder question: does an attacker even need access to the specific system being attacked? They trained a student model only against MinusFace, but with a twist — they replaced MinusFace's actual conversion process with a generic simple high-pass filter, something any attacker could construct without insider knowledge of MinusFace's internals. That model was then tested, cold, against templates produced by PartialFace and FracFace, systems it never saw during training. Table VI reports the results: against FracFace, Face++ success at five attempts was 0.946, Amazon API success was 0.473, and matching accuracy was 0.949. Against PartialFace, the numbers were 0.946, 0.447, and 0.925. These are lower than the matched-access numbers from earlier sections, but still recover a substantial share of identities from systems the model was never trained on. The natural reading is that all three frequency-domain schemes share a structural weakness — their protected outputs remain strongly coupled to simple high-pass filtering, regardless of the specific shuffling or channel-selection scheme layered on top. This transfer route is inherently a frequency-domain phenomenon and doesn't extend to CanFG, a non-frequency system, though CanFG remains vulnerable under the standard oracle-access model. A final variant relaxes one more assumption: rather than the attacker observing two templates built from the same secret channel-selection configuration, each training sample instead draws its own fresh random channel subset, averaged into a single-channel image, so no shared secret ever appears. Identity recovery still holds up substantially, indicating the fixed secret components were never load-bearing for the vulnerability in the first place.

[Beyond PPFR: breaking face de-identification and anonymization systems]
Everything so far has been about protecting recognition -- templates a machine can still match but a human can't reconstruct into a face. Now flip the goal. A separate line of work wants the opposite: keep the image looking like the same person to a human, while making automated face recognition fail on it. This is the face de-identification and anonymization literature, and the paper tests three representative systems. TIP-IM works by adversarial perturbation -- small, imperceptible pixel noise pushing the image's embedding away from the true identity in feature space. PerceptFace is utility-preserving and synthesis-based, generating a new face a viewer would call the same person but that scores as a mismatch to a recognizer. CanFG is different again: it explicitly conflates the two problems, building a deterministic one-to-one mapping from each identity to a substitute virtual identity. The authors test two stages -- CanFG-Ano, where the physical identity is stripped, and full CanFG, where the mapped virtual identity is then injected back in, which they suspect is more vulnerable since that injection reintroduces a stable identity signal to latch onto.

The argument for why this family should also be breakable is symmetric to the main thesis: these systems must leave enough identity information for a human to recognize the face, and that's exactly the signal the same distillation pipeline can extract. So they ran the identical training recipe -- two thousand protected-original pairs from FFHQ512 -- but swapped in AdaFace as the attacker's embedding backbone instead of whatever backbone each protection method targets, to avoid inflating success by attacking a system with its own reference model. Evaluation used LFW: 10,723 clean gallery images across 5,454 identities, with 1,550 protected query images, scored with the same identity-level retrieval metrics as before.

The results, in Table VIII, are stark. Top-1 recall goes from 0.0606 to 0.9271 for PerceptFace, from 0.1516 to 0.7916 for CanFG, from 0.0516 to 0.8535 for CanFG-Ano, and from 0.4761 to 0.9535 for TIP-IM -- all closing in on the clean-query upper bound of 0.9594. TIP-IM's pre-attack number is already unusually high; the authors attribute that to a backbone mismatch, since it was optimized against ArcFace while the attack uses AdaFace, so its protection was arguably weak from the start.

Table IX shows how little data this needs. At just 100 pairs, PerceptFace's Recall@1 already jumps from 0.0606 to 0.6148, and Recall@0.5% to 0.8865. That matters because PerceptFace's own defense is to rate-limit how many query pairs an attacker can obtain. The paper's response is that this is weak in practice, since these systems are openly accessible services -- nothing stops repeated querying. Across the methods tested, meaningful leakage shows up by around 200 samples.

[Soft biometric leakage and conclusion]
Beyond hard identity, there's a second axis of leakage the paper insists on treating separately: soft biometric attributes. Skin color and race, age, and gender are not identity per se, but they're sensitive personal data in their own right, and privacy law treats them that way explicitly — the paper points to Canada's Privacy Act, which names race and age as protected categories. So a protected template that leaks these attributes is a privacy failure even if it never leaks who the person is.

This section is partly a rebuttal to a rebuttal. When FracFace's authors responded to reviewers on OpenReview, they claimed their scheme successfully obfuscates age, gender, and ethnicity, and backed it with a human-perception study: under 13 percent of participants said they used these cues to infer identity, and over 76 percent admitted they were essentially guessing. That's a subjective, survey-based defense. The authors here push back with a direct observation — look at Figure 1, the regenerated faces from their own attack pipeline. Skin tone, apparent age, apparent gender are all visibly intact. A human study about what people say they relied on doesn't establish that the information isn't recoverable computationally.

So they quantify it. They take a CNN, attach a multi-head prediction MLP on top, and train it for a single epoch on FairFace — a dataset labeled for age, gender, and race across seven categories — then evaluate on 10,000 images. Table X reports race accuracy, gender accuracy, and age mean absolute error in years, for templates from each protected system. FracFace comes in at 0.673 race accuracy, 0.925 gender accuracy, 4.737 years age error. PartialFace is nearly identical: 0.663, 0.932, 4.952. Compare that to the original, unprotected image as an upper bound — 0.700, 0.949, 4.584 — and the gap is almost nothing. These two systems are barely suppressing soft biometrics at all. MinusFace does better, at 0.569, 0.893, 5.883, but it's still far above the random baseline of 0.146, 0.493, and 21.51 years — so meaningfully less protection than it might appear from pixel metrics alone. CanFG was left out of this particular test, but not because it passed — the opposite: it's designed to retain soft biometrics on purpose, so testing for leakage there would be meaningless.

The conclusion draws all of this together. Across three frequency-domain PPFR systems, the same distillation pipeline — no pixel reconstruction required — recovers identity at 98.5 percent matching accuracy with regeneration success above 96 percent, and against de-identification systems it restores identity-level top-1 retrieval recall on LFW to between 0.79 and 0.95 using 2,000 paired samples, against a clean-query ceiling of 0.96, with meaningful leakage already visible from just a few hundred pairs. The recommendation is procedural rather than a new defense: adopt identity-centric evaluation alongside pixel metrics as standard practice, and treat cryptographic approaches as the more promising direction for actual, provable privacy guarantees.

[Outro]
That's the paper: three frequency-domain protection systems broken not by rebuilding photos but by distilling identity directly out of their templates, the same approach turned against de-identification systems built for the opposite goal, and a look at how much age, gender, and race information survives protection that was never really measuring for it. The recommendation isn't a new defense — it's a change in what counts as evidence: identity-centric evaluation alongside pixel metrics, and a shift toward cryptographic approaches, where the property that actually matters, provable non-invertibility, can be stated and proven rather than assumed from a PSNR score.
